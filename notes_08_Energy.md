# Lecture 08: Energy

## Technical Terms & Definitions

**Low Power**
- Minimizing power consumption (P = V×I) measured in Watts
- Important for power supply design, PCB design, and short-term cooling requirements
- Relevant for maximum current delivery and heat dissipation

**Low Energy**
- Minimizing total energy consumption measured in Joules (Energy = Power × Time)
- Important for battery dimension, long-term temperature effects, and energy costs
- Critical for battery-operated and energy harvesting systems

**Static Power**
- Power consumed when device is powered-up but not actively switching
- Caused by leakage currents: gate-oxide leakage, subthreshold leakage, junction leakage
- Increasingly important as transistor sizes decrease (see graph showing static power becoming dominant after 2015)

**Dynamic Power**
- Power consumed during operation/switching
- Formula: **P = α×C×V²×F**
- Caused by charging/discharging capacitors and glitches (short circuit paths during switching)

**Power Gating**
- Technique to cut-off power supply to inactive units
- Reduces static power consumption by completely powering down unused logic blocks
- Implemented using transistor switches in VDD or ground paths

**Dynamic Voltage Scaling (DVS)**
- Technique to adjust supply voltage dynamically
- Very effective: lowering voltage has squared effect on power consumption (P ∝ V²)
- Must consider voltage-frequency relationships for stable operation

**Dynamic Frequency Scaling (DFS)**
- Also known as CPU throttling
- Adjusting clock frequency to reduce power (P ∝ F)
- Linear effect on power consumption

**Clock Gating**
- Technique to disable clock signals to unused peripherals/modules
- Reduces dynamic power by preventing unnecessary switching

**Low Power Mode (LPM)**
- Special operating states where parts of MCU are shut down
- Different levels (LPM0-LPM4) with varying degrees of power savings and wake-up times
- Trade-off between power savings and responsiveness

**Dynamic Power Management (DPM)**
- Strategy for transitioning between power states
- Key parameters: Tsd (shutdown delay), Tw (waiting time), Twu (wakeup delay)
- Must consider break-even time: when energy savings exceed transition costs

**Energy Harvesting**
- Collecting energy from environment (solar, thermal, vibration, RF)
- System only runs when sufficient energy available
- Requires special software precautions for clean re-starts after power loss

**Consumption Patterns**
- How energy usage varies over time in different operational modes
- Important factor impacting battery life along with age and energy level

**Non-Dispersive Infrared (NDIR) Sensor**
- CO2 sensor technology using infrared light absorption
- Example: SCD30 (19 mA average, 75 mA peak at 3.3V)

**Metal Oxide (MOX) Sensor**
- Gas sensor requiring heating element
- Example: MH-Z19C (<200mW average, requires preheat time)

**Photoacoustic Spectroscopy (PAS)**
- CO2 detection using sound waves generated by light absorption
- Example: Infineon XENSIV (30 mW average power consumption)

**Secure Element**
- Integrated circuit for executing cryptographic algorithms in hardware
- Example from Ultra96: Configuration and Security Unit (CSU)
- Significant power variation between manufacturers for same operations

**DRAM (Dynamic RAM)**
- Volatile memory using charge on capacitor (1T1C cell)
- Requires periodic refresh
- Power consumption varies with access patterns

**SRAM (Static RAM)**
- Volatile memory using interlocked state of logic gates (6T cell)
- Faster than DRAM but more power-hungry during active use

**FeRAM (Ferroelectric RAM)**
- Non-volatile memory using ferroelectric capacitor polarization
- Lower write energy than DRAM/SRAM

**MRAM (Magnetoresistive RAM)**
- Non-volatile memory using magnetization of ferromagnetic layer (1T1R cell)

**Energy Model**
- Mathematical or empirical representation predicting energy consumption
- Used in early design phases before hardware available
- Based on datasheets, profiling tools, static analysis

**Break-Even Time**
- Minimum idle time where transitioning to low-power mode saves energy
- Must account for: energy cost of shutdown + energy during sleep + energy cost of wakeup
- Formula: Tbe = (Esd + Ewu) / (Pactive - Psleep)

**Switching Activity (α)**
- Percentage of transistors switching per clock cycle
- Key parameter in dynamic power formula
- Reduced through algorithmic optimization and clock gating

**Capacitive Load (C)**
- Total capacitance being charged/discharged
- Includes gate capacitance, interconnect capacitance, load capacitance
- Reduced through smaller transistors and shorter interconnects

**Compiler Optimization Levels**
- -O0: No optimization (highest energy)
- -O1, -O2, -O3: Increasing optimization
- -Ofast, -Os: Speed and size optimization (often lowest energy)
- Example: Pbrt program: 556.2J at -O0 vs 134.4J at -Ofast (76% reduction)

**WFI (Wait For Interrupt)**
- ARM instruction to enter sleep mode
- CPU waits in low-power state until interrupt occurs
- Automatically exits when interrupt received

**Power Analyzer**
- Instrument providing constant voltage supply with unlimited current capability
- Measures voltage and current simultaneously with high precision
- 4-wire (Kelvin) sensing compensates for cable resistance

**Shunt Resistor**
- Low-value precision resistor in series with load
- Voltage across resistor proportional to current: I = V/R
- Must be sized to minimize voltage drop while maintaining measurement accuracy

## Core Concepts

### Power vs. Energy Distinction
Understanding the difference between power and energy optimization is fundamental:
- **Power (Watts)**: Rate of energy consumption at a given moment
  - Critical for: power supply sizing, PCB design, cooling requirements
  - Formula: P = V × I

- **Energy (Joules)**: Total consumption over time
  - Critical for: battery sizing, operational lifetime, total cost
  - Formula: E = P × t = V × I × t

A system can have low average power but high peak power, or vice versa. Both must be considered.

### Static vs. Dynamic Power Consumption

**Static Power Sources:**
- Gate oxide leakage through thin insulating layers
- Subthreshold leakage when transistor is "off" but still conducting
- Junction leakage at PN junctions
- Trend: Static power becoming more significant as transistors shrink (graph shows crossover around 2010)

**Dynamic Power Sources:**
- Switching currents from charging/discharging capacitances
- Short-circuit currents (glitches) when both PMOS and NMOS momentarily conduct
- Formula: Pdynamic = α × C × V² × F
  - α: switching activity factor
  - C: capacitive load
  - V: supply voltage
  - F: operating frequency

**Optimization strategies differ:**
- Static: power gating, low-power modes, reduce leakage
- Dynamic: voltage scaling, frequency scaling, reduce switching activity

### Abstraction Levels in Energy Analysis

Energy consumption must be analyzed at multiple levels:

1. **System Level**
   - HW: Components and subsystems
   - SW: Tasks describing system functionality
   - Example: Traffic light system with multiple modules

2. **Module Level**
   - HW: Processors, MCUs, ASICs, buses, peripherals, memory, storage
   - SW: Interacting software modules
   - Most accessible level for energy optimization

3. **Block Level**
   - HW: Counters, registers, ALUs
   - SW: Program sequences, loops
   - Architectural optimization level

4. **Logic Level**
   - HW: Logic gates, flip-flops
   - SW: Individual instructions (e.g., ADDS R1, R2, R3)
   - Detailed but requires hardware design knowledge

5. **Device Level**
   - HW: Electronic components, transistors
   - SW: Machine code (e.g., 0x21ff)
   - Lowest level, usually addressed by chip designers

### Component-Level Impact Analysis

**Traffic Light Example:**
System components:
- Traffic light modules (LEDs)
- Central control unit (MCU)
- Push buttons for pedestrians
- Automatic car detection system
- Internet connection box

Question: Which component uses most energy?
Answer: LED modules (continuous operation at high power) dominate over MCU and sensors

**Optimization Priority:**
Focus optimization efforts where energy consumption is highest. Use Pareto principle: 80% of energy consumption typically comes from 20% of components.

### MCU Low Power Modes

**Hierarchical Power States:**
- **Active Mode**: All systems operational
  - Highest power consumption
  - Full CPU speed and peripheral access

- **Sleep Mode**: CPU stopped, peripherals running
  - Medium power savings
  - Fast wake-up (no state loss)
  - Example STM32F429: CPU off, peripherals active

- **Stop Mode**: All clocks stopped
  - High power savings
  - Moderate wake-up time
  - Registers retained

- **Standby Mode**: Power domain off
  - Maximum power savings
  - Slow wake-up, state lost
  - Example STM32F429: 1.2V domain powered off

**TI MSP430F5xxx Example:**
- LPM0: CPU disabled, all clocks active
- LPM1: CPU disabled, ACLK and SMCLK active, DCO disabled
- LPM2: CPU disabled, ACLK active, SMCLK and DCO disabled
- LPM3: CPU disabled, only ACLK active
- LPM4: CPU disabled, all clocks disabled, crystal oscillator stopped
- LPM3.5: Like LPM3 but with internal regulator disabled (lowest power)

**Power Consumption Examples (MSP430, 3V):**
- Active (8 MHz): ~2.4 mA
- LPM0: ~1.9 mA
- LPM3: ~2.0 μA
- LPM3.5: ~0.8 μA (1000× reduction!)
- LPM4: ~0.9 μA

**Temperature Impact:**
Power consumption increases significantly with temperature:
- MSP430 LPM0 at 25°C: 83 μA (typ)
- MSP430 LPM0 at 85°C: 94 μA (typ)
- Important for systems operating in harsh environments

### Dynamic Power Management Strategy

**State Transition Diagram:**
```
Application States: [busy] → [waiting] → [busy]
Power States:      [run] → [Tsd|sleep|Twu] → [run]
```

**Key Timing Parameters:**
- **Tsd**: Shutdown delay (time to enter sleep mode)
- **Tw**: Waiting time (duration in sleep mode)
- **Twu**: Wakeup delay (time to return to active mode)

**Energy Trade-off Calculation:**
```
Eactive = Pactive × (Tsd + Tw + Twu)
Esleep = Pactive×Tsd + Psleep×Tw + Pactive×Twu

Energy saved = Eactive - Esleep = (Pactive - Psleep) × Tw - (Pactive×(Tsd + Twu))
```

Break-even occurs when: Tw ≥ (Tsd + Twu) × Pactive / (Pactive - Psleep)

**Data Retention Consideration:**
Deep sleep modes (e.g., MSP430 LPM3.5) may not retain RAM:
- Additional code needed to save/restore state
- Trade-off: code complexity vs. energy savings
- Must evaluate if energy saved during Tw exceeds overhead energy

### Software Optimization Techniques

**Task Scheduling:**
- Minimize CPU active time
- Use event-driven architecture instead of polling
- Batch operations to reduce wake-up frequency
- Example: Instead of checking sensor every 100ms, use interrupt-driven approach

**Data Transmission Optimization:**
- Use power-efficient protocols (BLE vs. WiFi)
- Compress data before transmission
- Use burst transmission instead of continuous streaming
- Example: Send 100 bytes once vs. 1 byte 100 times (latter uses much more energy)

**Sensor Fusion:**
- Combine data from multiple sensors in single wake-up
- Reduce total number of CPU activations
- Example: Read temperature, humidity, pressure in one transaction vs. three separate transactions

**Energy-Efficient Algorithms:**
- Minimize computational complexity
- Use efficient data structures (arrays vs. linked lists)
- Reduce memory access (cache-friendly algorithms)
- Avoid unnecessary floating-point operations

**Low-Power Software Design Patterns:**
- Eliminate busy-wait loops: `while(!flag);` → interrupt-driven
- Minimize polling frequency
- Use hardware timers instead of software delays
- Disable unused peripherals in software

**Compiler Optimization:**
Energy consumption varies significantly by optimization level:
- -O0 (no optimization): baseline
- -O3 (aggressive optimization): typically 30-50% energy reduction
- -Ofast (speed optimization): often best energy efficiency
- -Os (size optimization): good for memory-constrained systems

### Energy Modeling and Early Estimation

**Purpose of Energy Models:**
- Verify energy NFRs during design phase (V-model left side)
- Compare design alternatives before implementation
- Predict battery lifetime
- Identify optimization opportunities early

**Model Building Approaches:**

1. **Datasheet-Based (Bottom-Up):**
   - Extract typical/maximum power values from datasheets
   - Consider operating modes and duty cycles
   - Example calculation for PAS CO2 sensor:
     ```
     Powerup: 29.7 mW × 0.8s = 23.76 mJ
     Measurement: 26.4 mW × 0.7s = 18.48 mJ
     Measurement@12V: 840 mW × 0.2s = 168 mJ
     Total per measurement = 210.24 mJ
     ```

2. **Profiling-Based (Measurement-Driven):**
   - Use power profiling tools during development
   - Measure actual consumption of code sections
   - Refine models with real data
   - Update models as implementation progresses

3. **Static Analysis:**
   - Analyze code to predict execution paths
   - Estimate instruction energy costs
   - Model memory access patterns
   - Combine with datasheet parameters

**Excel-Based System Model:**
Simple spreadsheet approach:
- List all components (MCU, sensors, communication, LEDs, etc.)
- For each component: Power consumption × Duty cycle
- Sum total average power
- Calculate battery lifetime: Battery capacity (mAh) / Average current (mA)

**Model Refinement Cycle:**
1. Create initial model from datasheets
2. Implement design
3. Measure actual consumption
4. Update model with measurements
5. Use refined model for next design iteration

### Energy Measurement Techniques

**Measurement Challenges:**
- Wide dynamic range (μA in sleep mode, mA in active mode)
- Fast transients during mode changes
- Need for high sampling rate to capture spikes
- Long measurement duration for accurate averaging
- Multiple power rails to monitor

**Accuracy Considerations:**
- Measurement equipment resolution and bandwidth
- Cable and connector resistance
- Temperature effects
- Supply voltage stability during measurement

**Operational State Coverage:**
Must measure all relevant states:
- Boot/initialization
- Active operation (various modes)
- Sleep modes (various levels)
- Transitions between states
- Worst-case scenarios

## Formulas & Procedures

### Power and Energy Fundamentals

**Basic Power Formula:**
```
P = V × I
```
- P: Power in Watts
- V: Voltage in Volts
- I: Current in Amperes

**Energy Calculation:**
```
E = P × t = V × I × t
```
- E: Energy in Joules
- t: Time in seconds

**Energy from Capacity:**
```
E = V × Q = V × (I × t)
```
- Q: Charge in Coulombs (or Ampere-hours)

### Dynamic Power Formula

**Complete Formula:**
```
P_dynamic = α × C × V² × F
```
- α (alpha): Switching activity factor (0 to 1)
- C: Capacitive load in Farads
- V: Supply voltage in Volts
- F: Operating frequency in Hz

**Component Analysis:**
- To reduce by 2×: decrease V by √2 (most effective due to V²)
- To reduce by 2×: decrease F by 2× (linear effect)
- To reduce by 2×: decrease α by 2× (linear effect, achievable through clock gating)

**Power Reduction Strategies:**
1. **Voltage scaling**: Most effective (squared relationship)
2. **Frequency scaling**: Linear reduction
3. **Reduce switching activity**: Software/algorithm optimization
4. **Reduce capacitance**: Hardware design (smaller transistors, shorter wires)

### Energy Calculation for Low Power Modes

**Active Mode Energy:**
```
E_active = P_active × t_total
```

**Sleep Mode Energy:**
```
E_sleep = P_active × T_sd + P_sleep × T_w + P_active × T_wu
```
- T_sd: Shutdown delay (time to enter sleep)
- T_w: Wait time (time in sleep mode)
- T_wu: Wakeup delay (time to return to active)

**Energy Saved:**
```
E_saved = E_active - E_sleep
E_saved = (P_active - P_sleep) × T_w - P_active × (T_sd + T_wu)
```

**Break-Even Time (Minimum sleep duration to save energy):**
```
T_break-even = (P_active × (T_sd + T_wu)) / (P_active - P_sleep)
```

**Example Calculation:**
Given:
- P_active = 50 mW
- P_sleep = 1 mW
- T_sd = 1 ms
- T_wu = 2 ms

Break-even time:
```
T_be = (50 × (1 + 2)) / (50 - 1) = 150 / 49 ≈ 3.06 ms
```
Must sleep for >3.06 ms to save energy

### Battery Lifetime Calculation

**Simple Lifetime Estimation:**
```
Lifetime (hours) = Battery_capacity (mAh) / Average_current (mA)
```

**With Duty Cycle:**
```
I_average = I_active × duty_cycle + I_sleep × (1 - duty_cycle)
```

**Example:**
Battery: 1000 mAh
Active mode: 50 mA for 1% of time
Sleep mode: 0.5 mA for 99% of time

```
I_avg = 50 × 0.01 + 0.5 × 0.99 = 0.5 + 0.495 = 0.995 mA
Lifetime = 1000 / 0.995 ≈ 1005 hours ≈ 42 days
```

Without sleep mode:
```
Lifetime = 1000 / 50 = 20 hours
```
Sleep mode extends life by 50×!

### Current Measurement with Shunt Resistor

**Ohm's Law Application:**
```
V_shunt = I_load × R_shunt
I_load = V_shunt / R_shunt
```

**Power Dissipation in Shunt:**
```
P_shunt = I_load² × R_shunt
```

**Example:**
- R_shunt = 1Ω
- I_load = 50 mA

```
V_shunt = 0.05 × 1 = 0.05 V = 50 mV
P_shunt = 0.05² × 1 = 0.0025 W = 2.5 mW
```

**Shunt Selection Criteria:**
- Low enough resistance to minimize voltage drop
- High enough resistance for measurable voltage
- Power rating adequate for worst-case current
- Low temperature coefficient for accuracy

### CO2 Sensor Energy Calculation (PAS Example)

**From Datasheet (Slide 35):**
- Power-up phase: 3.3V, 9 mA, 0.8s
- Measurement phase: 3.3V, 8 mA, 0.7s
- Measurement @12V: 12V, 70 mA, 0.2s

**Energy per Measurement:**
```
E_powerup = 3.3V × 9mA × 0.8s = 23.76 mJ
E_meas_3.3V = 3.3V × 8mA × 0.7s = 18.48 mJ
E_meas_12V = 12V × 70mA × 0.2s = 168 mJ
E_total = 23.76 + 18.48 + 168 = 210.24 mJ per measurement
```

**Annual Energy (1 measurement/hour):**
```
E_annual = 210.24 mJ × 24 × 365 = 1,841,702 mJ ≈ 1.84 kJ
```

**Battery Requirements (AAA battery ≈ 1000 mAh @ 1.5V):**
```
E_battery = 1.5V × 1000mAh × 3600s/h = 5400 J
Can support: 5400 / 0.21024 ≈ 25,686 measurements
At 1/hour: 25,686 hours ≈ 3 years
```

### Voltage and Frequency Scaling Relationships

**General Voltage-Frequency Constraint:**
```
F_max ∝ (V_DD - V_threshold) / V_DD
```

For CMOS circuits, maximum frequency depends on supply voltage. Cannot arbitrarily reduce voltage without reducing frequency.

**Typical Relationships (example values):**
- 3.3V → 25 MHz maximum
- 2.2V → 12 MHz maximum
- 1.8V → 8 MHz maximum

**Combined DVFS Power Reduction:**
```
P_new / P_old = (V_new / V_old)² × (F_new / F_old)
```

Example: Reduce from 3.3V/24MHz to 2.2V/12MHz:
```
P_new / P_old = (2.2/3.3)² × (12/24) = 0.445 × 0.5 = 0.22
Power reduced to 22% (78% savings!)
```

### Energy Profiling Procedure

**Step-by-Step Measurement Process:**

1. **Setup Phase:**
   - Connect power analyzer with 4-wire sensing
   - Isolate all power sources (check for USB power, debug probe power)
   - Set appropriate voltage and current limits
   - Verify stable power supply

2. **Baseline Measurement:**
   - Measure system in reset/idle state
   - Record quiescent current
   - Identify any unexpected power draw

3. **Functional Measurement:**
   - Run target application/test scenario
   - Capture complete power profile including:
     - Boot/initialization
     - Active operation
     - Sleep modes
     - Mode transitions
   - Record at high sample rate (>10 kHz) to catch transients

4. **Analysis:**
   - Calculate average power for each state
   - Identify peak currents
   - Measure state duration
   - Compute energy per operation
   - Compare with energy model

5. **Iteration:**
   - Identify high-energy operations
   - Optimize software/hardware
   - Re-measure to verify improvement
   - Update energy model with actual data

## Comparisons & Contrasts

### Low Power vs. Low Energy

| Aspect | Low Power | Low Energy |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Definition** | Minimize instantaneous power consumption (P = V×I) | Minimize total energy consumption over time (E = P×t) |
| **Units** | Watts (W) or milliwatts (mW) | Joules (J) or watt-hours (Wh) |
| **Design Focus** | Peak/instantaneous demand | Average consumption over operating period |
| **Primary Concerns** | - Power supply sizing<br>- PCB design (trace width)<br>- Cooling/heat dissipation<br>- Voltage drop | - Battery dimension/capacity<br>- Operating lifetime<br>- Long-term temperature effects<br>- Energy costs |
| **Example Priority** | Desktop computer (constant mains power, cooling critical) | Wearable device (battery life critical, low peak power acceptable) |
| **Optimization** | Reduce voltage, limit current spikes, manage heat | Maximize sleep time, minimize active duration, optimize duty cycle |
| **Measurement** | Power meter, oscilloscope + current probe | Energy integrating meter, long-term logging |

**Key Insight:** A system can have low average power but high peak power (intermittent high loads) or high average power with low peaks (continuous moderate loads). Both must be considered in design.

### Static Power vs. Dynamic Power

| Characteristic | Static Power | Dynamic Power |
|----------------|--------------|---------------|
| **When Consumed** | Always, when powered (even if idle) | During switching/operation only |
| **Main Causes** | - Gate oxide leakage<br>- Subthreshold leakage<br>- Junction leakage | - Capacitance charging/discharging<br>- Short-circuit currents (glitches) |
| **Formula** | P_static ≈ V_DD × I_leakage | P_dynamic = α×C×V²×F |
| **Technology Trend** | Increasing (smaller transistors → more leakage) | Decreasing (lower voltages, smaller capacitances) |
| **Dominant Era** | Post-2015 for sub-28nm processes | Pre-2010 for larger process nodes |
| **Mitigation HW** | - Power gating<br>- Multi-threshold transistors<br>- Body biasing | - Voltage scaling<br>- Frequency scaling<br>- Smaller capacitances |
| **Mitigation SW** | - Enter deep sleep modes<br>- Power down unused blocks | - Clock gating<br>- Reduce switching activity<br>- Optimize algorithms |
| **Temperature Dependence** | Strong (exponential increase with temp) | Weak (slight variation with temp) |

**Historical Note:** Around 2010-2015, static and dynamic power crossed over for advanced process nodes. Modern designs must address both equally.

| Mode | CPU | Clocks Active | Typical Current (3V) | Wake-up Time | State Retention | Use Case |

| **Active** | Running | All (MCLK, SMCLK, ACLK) | 2.4 mA @ 8MHz | N/A | Full | Normal operation |

### Energy Harvesting vs. Battery vs. Mains Power

| Aspect | Energy Harvesting | Battery Powered | Mains Powered |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Power Budget** | Variable (μW to mW) | Fixed capacity (hours to years) | Unlimited |
| **Design Challenge** | Operate only when energy available | Maximize lifetime | Minimize heat/cost |
| **Energy Storage** | Required (supercapacitor/small battery) | Battery is storage | N/A |
| **Software Strategy** | - Graceful shutdown on low power<br>- Clean restart after power loss<br>- Opportunistic operation | - Deep sleep modes<br>- Duty cycling<br>- Event-driven architecture | - Optional low-power modes<br>- Focus on performance |
| **Hardware Strategy** | - Ultra-low-power components<br>- Efficient energy conversion<br>- Power good detection | - Efficient voltage regulation<br>- Low-leakage components<br>- Optimized BOM | - Standard components<br>- Reliable power supply |
| **Predictability** | Low (depends on environment) | Medium (aging affects capacity) | High (stable supply) |
| **Maintenance** | None (if reliable) | Battery replacement required | None (except failures) |
| **Cost** | High (harvester + storage) | Medium (battery cost) | Low (power supply) |
| **Use Cases** | Wireless sensors, remote monitoring | Wearables, IoT, portable devices | Industrial, desktop, always-on systems |

**Critical Differences:**

**Energy Harvesting Considerations:**
- Must handle sudden power loss mid-operation
- Need non-volatile storage or quick state save
- May operate intermittently
- Example: Solar-powered sensor sleeps during night, operates during day

**Battery Consumption Patterns Impact:**
- Lithium batteries: performance degrades with age
- High current pulses reduce effective capacity
- Temperature affects performance (cold = less capacity)
- Depth of discharge affects cycle life

**Mains-Powered Low Energy Benefits:**
- Lower energy bills
- Reduced cooling costs
- Extended component life (lower temperature)
- Environmental responsibility

### Example 3: Dynamic Power Management Decision

**Scenario:** MCU waiting for external event with uncertain timing (could be 1ms to 1000ms)

**System Parameters:**
- Active power: 30 mW
- Sleep power: 0.1 mW
- Transition to sleep: 100 μs @ 30 mW = 3 μJ
- Wakeup from sleep: 200 μs @ 30 mW = 6 μJ
- Total overhead: 9 μJ

**Break-even Calculation:**
```
E_active(t) = 30 mW × t
E_sleep(t) = 30 mW × 0.0001s + 0.1 mW × t + 30 mW × 0.0002s
E_overhead = 3 μJ + 6 μJ = 9 μJ

Break-even when E_sleep = E_active:
9 μJ + 0.1 mW × t = 30 mW × t
9 μJ = 29.9 mW × t
t = 9 μJ / 29.9 mW = 0.3 ms
```

**Decision Strategy:**
- If expected wait < 0.3 ms → Stay active (busy-wait or short sleep)
- If expected wait > 0.3 ms → Enter sleep mode
- If unknown but likely > 1 ms → Enter sleep (safe choice)

**Energy Comparison for 10ms wait:**
```
Active approach: 30 mW × 10 ms = 300 μJ
Sleep approach: 9 μJ + 0.1 mW × 10 ms = 10 μJ
Savings: 290 μJ (97% reduction)
```

**Implementation:**
```c
void wait_for_event(uint32_t timeout_ms) {
    if (timeout_ms > 1) {  // More than 1ms, worth sleeping
        enter_sleep_mode();
        // Will wake on interrupt or timeout
    } else {
        // Short wait, busy loop with WFI
        __WFI();  // Light sleep, fast wake
    }
}
```

**Lesson:** Break-even analysis guides sleep mode decisions. When in doubt with longer waits, sleep saves energy.

### Example 4: STM32F429 Temperature Sensor Application

**Application:** Room temperature monitoring system

**Functional Requirements:**
- Read temperature sensor every 10 seconds
- Display on LCD (update only on change >0.5°C)
- Send data via UART every minute
- Button press wakes for immediate reading

**Initial Design (Poor Energy Profile):**
```c
// Naive implementation - always active
while(1) {
    temp = read_temperature();  // 50ms
    if (abs(temp - last_temp) > 0.5) {
        update_lcd(temp);  // 20ms
    }
    if (time_since_uart > 60000) {
        send_uart(temp);  // 10ms
        time_since_uart = 0;
    }
    HAL_Delay(10000);  // Busy wait!
}
```

**Power consumption:** ~50 mA continuous (MCU always active)

**Optimized Design (Low Energy Profile):**
```c
// Configure for lowest power
void enter_stop_mode(void) {
    // Disable LCD backlight
    HAL_GPIO_WritePin(LCD_BL_GPIO, GPIO_PIN_RESET);

    // Enter STOP mode
    HAL_PWR_EnterSTOPMode(PWR_LOWPOWERREGULATOR_ON, PWR_STOPENTRY_WFI);

    // Wake-up: reconfigure clocks (done by HAL)
}

int main(void) {
    configure_rtc_wakeup(10);  // Wake every 10s
    configure_button_interrupt();

    while(1) {
        temp = read_temperature();  // 50ms active

        if (abs(temp - last_temp) > 0.5) {
            enable_lcd();
            update_lcd(temp);  // 20ms active
            disable_lcd();
        }

        if (time_since_uart >= 60) {
            send_uart(temp);  // 10ms active
            time_since_uart = 0;
        }

        enter_stop_mode();  // Sleep until next wakeup
    }
}
```

**Power Analysis:**

**Per 10-second cycle:**
```
Active time:
- Temperature read: 50 ms @ 50 mA = 2.5 mA·ms
- LCD update (10% of time): 20 ms @ 60 mA × 0.1 = 120 μA·ms
- UART (every 6th cycle): 10 ms @ 40 mA / 6 = 67 μA·ms
Total active: ~2.7 mA·ms

Sleep time: 9950 ms @ 10 μA = 99.5 μA·ms

Average current = (2.7 + 0.1) mA·ms / 10,000 ms ≈ 0.28 mA
```

**Comparison:**
- Naive: 50 mA → 220 mAh battery lasts 4.4 hours
- Optimized: 0.28 mA → 220 mAh battery lasts 785 hours (33 days)

**Improvement: 178× battery life extension!**

**Key Techniques:**
1. Sleep mode between readings (99.5% of time)
2. Conditional LCD updates (reduce active time)
3. Power down LCD backlight when not needed
4. Interrupt-driven wake-up (no busy-waiting)


**Error reduced from 73% to 15%** (acceptable for early estimates)

**Remaining discrepancy likely:**
- Temperature effects
- Component tolerance
- Measurement uncertainty
- Other unmodeled factors (GPIO transitions, etc.)

**Lessons:**
1. Initial models are typically 30-100% off
2. Iterate: measure → analyze → update model
3. Don't forget support circuitry (regulators, pull-ups, LEDs)
4. Datasheets give typical values, but expect variation
5. Transition times and overheads accumulate

### Example 8: Multi-Level Optimization Strategy

**Product:** Handheld medical device

**Requirements:**
- Battery life: 8-hour shift
- Response time: <100 ms for UI
- Display always visible
- Wireless communication (occasional)

**Optimization Levels:**

**Level 1: System Architecture (before implementation)**
- Selected low-power OLED (25 mW) instead of LCD backlight (200 mW)
- Energy savings: 175 mW continuous → 42 J per hour
- Chose BLE over WiFi: 15 mW avg vs 250 mW avg when active
- Selected efficient DCDC converter: 90% efficiency vs 75%

**Level 2: Component Selection**
- MCU: STM32L4 (low-power series)
  - Active: 100 μA/MHz
  - Stop mode: 1 μA
- Sensors: Digital with sleep modes instead of analog (no continuous power for ADC reference)

**Level 3: Software Architecture**
- Event-driven design (no busy loops)
- Hierarchical state machine with power-aware states
- Deferred processing: batch operations during active periods

**Level 4: Implementation Details**
- Compiler optimization: -O3 for all code
- DMA for data transfers (CPU can sleep)
- Optimal sleep mode selection:
  - UI idle >100ms: Sleep mode (wake on button IRQ)
  - Between sensor readings: Stop mode
  - Never use Standby (wake-up too slow for <100ms response)

**Level 5: Runtime Optimization**
- Dynamic display brightness based on ambient light
- Adaptive wireless packet aggregation (batch transmissions)
- Predictive wake-up (start waking before expected event)

**Results:**

**Baseline (poor design):**
```
- LCD backlight: 200 mW
- WiFi: 250 mW @ 10% = 25 mW
- MCU always active: 50 mW
- Sensors always on: 10 mW
Total: 285 mW → Battery life: 2.1 hours (FAILED!)
```

**After Level 1-2 optimizations:**
```
- OLED: 25 mW
- BLE: 15 mW @ 5% = 0.75 mW
- MCU: (estimated) 20 mW
- Sensors: 2 mW avg
Total: 48 mW → Battery life: 12.5 hours (Exceeds target)
```

**After Level 3-5 optimizations:**
```
- OLED: 25 mW (unchanged)
- BLE: 0.5 mW (batching)
- MCU: 5 mW (deep sleep 95% of time)
- Sensors: 0.5 mW (sleep modes)
Total: 31 mW → Battery life: 19.4 hours (2.4× target!)
```

**Savings Breakdown:**
- Architecture choices: 237 mW (83% of baseline)
- Component selection: (included in architecture)
- Software optimization: 17 mW (6% of baseline)
- Total reduction: 89% from baseline

**Lesson:** Architecture and component selection provide 80%+ of energy savings. Software optimization provides incremental improvements. Optimize at all levels for best results.

### Connection to Future Lecture 13: RTOS Scheduling
- **Tickless Idle**: RTOS can skip periodic timer interrupts during idle (save energy)

- **Heterogeneous Systems**: Mix of high-performance and low-power cores (big.LITTLE architecture)
- **Coherency Overhead**: Cache coherency protocols consume energy in multi-core systems

## Summary

Energy optimization in embedded systems requires a holistic approach spanning all design levels from system architecture to detailed implementation. Key principles include:

1. **Distinguish Power from Energy**: Optimize for what matters (battery life → energy, cooling → power)
2. **Design Early**: Energy considerations must start in requirements phase, not be retrofitted
3. **Multi-Level Approach**: Address energy at system, module, and implementation levels
4. **Measurement and Modeling**: Use models for early estimates, validate with measurements, iterate
5. **Component Selection**: Hardware choices often dominate (80%+ impact)
6. **Software Optimization**: Maximize sleep time, minimize active time, optimize active code
7. **Trade-offs**: Energy vs. performance, energy vs. cost, energy vs. responsiveness

The lecture provides practical techniques (LPMs, DVFS, DPM), measurement methods (power analyzers, shunt resistors), and real-world examples (sensors, secure elements, MCUs) to enable energy-aware embedded system design.
